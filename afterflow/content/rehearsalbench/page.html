Can AI run your company?

We gave ten AI models a simulated bank contact centre, built from real call records. Each one made a plan and predicted what it would do. We ran every plan 600 times.

Why does this matter?

In July 2025, the Commonwealth Bank of Australia said it would replace 45 customer-service roles with an AI voice bot. The union said call volumes were rising. In August the bank reversed the decision and admitted the roles “were not redundant.”

The plan rested on a forecast of what the bot would do to calls, and the forecast was wrong. AI models now make forecasts like that, and propose the plans too. We built RehearsalBench to test whether they get them right.

One future Rewind Evidence Plan Futures Score

A company only sees what happened.

When a company makes a change, it sees one result. It never finds out what the other options would have done, so a lucky call can look like a good one.

So we built contact centres you can rewind.

Fourteen simulated bank contact centres, built from real call records. We tested the engine on a second bank it had never seen.

The AI sees what a manager sees.

It reads the same reports a manager would, gaps included. The simulator's rules stay hidden.

It can ask for more data, but the bill comes out of the plan's value.

It makes a plan and a prediction.

It can reshape staffing, bring in AI tools or leave things alone. Each prediction comes with a range, and it's locked before anything runs.

Then every model predicts the same set of changes, so we can compare them head to head.

We let the future happen.

Each plan runs 200 times in three versions of the bank: as described, worse and better. Every model gets the same luck.

Then we compare.

A plan scores what it saves, at the bank's own valuations. Break the service floor and it scores zero.

A prediction scores on how close it came, and whether its range held the truth.

Every model made the same mistake.

The models made the same sizing errors. They expected a voice agent to raise repeat calls by about half a percentage point. It raised them by a tenth of one. Everyone overrated agent assist and callbacks too.

Finance wanted a 10% cut. The model said no.

On one bank, GPT-6 Astra read the hourly reports and found nobody answering weekday calls from 9 pm to midnight. 15,957 calls got an after-hours message.

It moved staff into those evenings and the morning peaks, and turned down the payroll cut in writing.

The plan was worth $156,683 more than doing nothing.

“The largest avoidable loss exposure is missing coverage, not long calls.”

Spending more buys little.

GPT-6.1 Sol nearly matched Astra's savings at a sixth of the price. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash saved 5.3% for 46 cents a run.

Price doesn't explain the bottom either. Gemini 3.8 Flash costs about what Sol does, but changed nothing on 75% of its runs and saved nothing. GPT-6 Luna made 5 changes per plan to Astra's 46, and left the bank worse off 43% of the time.

More findings

Nobody held off.

On two banks, nothing on offer reliably beat doing nothing. No model ever recommended holding off.

Each one manages differently.

Astra never used a voice agent. Mistral Small used one in 86% of its plans. Astra and Sol bought extra data about three times a run. Haiku hardly ever did, and its plans delivered less than it promised.

The models that did well here did well in reality.

A simulation is only worth anything if it tells you something about the real world, so we tested that too. Twelve models forecast 33 real changes where we knew the outcome, from software teams adopting AI coding tools to government contact centres.

The same five models lead both rankings, and the same three trail. The three in green joined after we froze the test, and still landed in the right order.

Better than price or popularity.

Ranking models by price puts 67% of pairs in the right order. LMArena gets 60%.
